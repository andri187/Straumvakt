# Dynamic grid communications in the EU, with focus on Scandinavia

**Research note · 5 August 2026**
Desk research from public sources. Evidence-classed per the convention in
[`README.md`](./README.md). Sources listed at the end.

---

## The headline: this is no longer a proposal, it is a legal framework obligation

**Directive (EU) 2024/1711** — the Electricity Market Design reform, in force
16 July 2024 — inserts **Article 6a into Directive (EU) 2019/944** requiring
that:

> "The regulatory authority or another competent authority where a Member
> State has so provided **shall develop a framework** for transmission system
> operators and distribution system operators to offer the possibility of
> establishing **flexible connection agreements** in areas where there is
> limited or no network capacity availability."

The framework must ensure flexible connections do not, as a rule, delay
reinforcement; that they convert to firm connections once the network is
developed; and that they may be permanent where reinforcement would be
inefficient. Agreements must specify **maximum firm and flexible injection and
withdrawal capacity**, applicable network charges, duration, and expected date
of full connection. System users connecting under one **must install certified
power control systems**.

**Transposition deadline: 17 January 2025 — already passed.** The Commission
opened infringement proceedings against 26 Member States in March 2025 for
incomplete transposition of this directive.

**The directive is marked "Text with EEA relevance."**

> ### What this changes for the Icelandic argument
>
> The technical report currently argues that dynamic connections *should*
> exist, using California and Australia as precedent. The stronger argument is
> that Europe has already legislated them, that the instrument is EEA-relevant,
> and that the transposition deadline has passed on the EU side.
>
> Three provisions map directly onto the Vík case:
> - **"maximum firm and flexible withdrawal capacity"** — precisely the
>   120 kW-firm / non-firm-remainder split in SOW §11
> - **"certified power control systems"** — the boundary controller and
>   fail-safe requirement
> - **"convert to firm once the network is developed"** — answers the
>   reasonable DSO objection that non-firm access becomes a permanent dodge

**Evidence class: Documented**, from the consolidated directive text.
**Not established by this research:** whether 2024/1711 has been incorporated
into the EEA Agreement by Joint Committee decision, and what Iceland's
implementation status is. Given the political history of the third energy
package in Iceland, this must be checked directly — with Utanríkisráðuneytið,
the EFTA Secretariat, or Raforkueftirlitið — before being asserted to RARIK.
*Do not claim it is binding on Iceland today.*

## Non-firm connections across Europe

**Adoption is already majority practice.** Non-firm/flexible connection
agreements are implemented in **15 of the 29 states** cooperating through ACER.
The EU Action Plan for Grids (2023) called for national frameworks; 2024/1711
made it an obligation.

Four models are distinguished in the literature:

| Model | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Capacity-limited** | Reduced guaranteed capacity for faster/cheaper connection |
| **Time-limited** | Access restricted to defined periods |
| **Dynamic operating envelopes** | Real-time limits varying with network conditions |
| **Fully flexible** | Access entirely conditional |

**Reported effect: connection costs reduced by up to 80%, lead times halved.**
That figure is from the literature rather than an audited study, and should be
cited as such — but even at a fraction of it, the economics dwarf the ISK
9.79 m/MW Landsnet capacity charge the report is built around.

**Evidence class: Documented / Inference on the applicability of the 80% figure
to Iceland.**

## Scandinavia, country by country

### The Nordic picture

A 2025 Nordic Energy Research study identified **45 demand-side flexibility
initiatives across the Nordics**, with ten studied in depth. Its headline
findings:

- **Conditional connections are used or piloted in all Nordic countries**
- **Norway is the most active** — around 20 demand-side flexibility projects
- **Sweden is the only Nordic country with permanent DSF solutions**, the rest
  being pilots
- Time-of-use and power-based grid tariffs are being implemented region-wide

### Norway — the furthest along on the connection question

Norway regulates conditional connection under the **NEM regulation
(Forskrift om nettregulering og energimarkedet)**, §3-2 and related provisions.
RME (the regulator, within NVE) proposed connection agreements with conditions
as an explicit **alternative to network investment**. A grid company and a
consumption customer may agree connection with terms for disconnection or
limitation, allowing faster connection while avoiding unnecessary investment.

Amendments took effect **1 January 2025**, with further changes from
**1 January 2026**. Connections ≥1 MW go through a maturity assessment and a
capacity queue, with grid companies obliged to register request, offer,
acceptance and capacity-availability dates.

Statnett publishes a standard framework agreement for connection on terms —
*Rammeavtale om tilknytning på vilkår* — meaning this is contractual boilerplate
in Norway, not a novel instrument.

**Relevance to Vík:** Norway has already solved the legal-instrument problem
Iceland has not started. A named regulation, a standard contract, and a queue
mechanism. This is the closest peer precedent available and far more persuasive
to RARIK than California.

### Sweden — permanent solutions and the flagship market

Sweden is the only Nordic country the study classes as having **permanent**
demand-side flexibility solutions rather than pilots.

**sthlmflex** is the flagship: Svenska kraftnät (TSO) with **Ellevio** and
**Vattenfall Eldistribution** (DSOs), **NODES** as independent market operator,
addressing the Stockholm winter capacity gap. It is described as the only
Swedish flexibility market testing **DSO–DSO** coordination as well as TSO–DSO,
and it enabled exchange of transmission capacity subscriptions between
connection nodes.

The Swedish regulator **Ei** commissioned a literature review on conditional
grid connections (EiR2023:08) as part of its work on *villkorade avtal*.

### Denmark — DSOs procuring flexibility to defer investment

**Cerius and Radius** together form Denmark's largest distribution network —
**1.4 million customers, over a third of Danish consumption**. They are testing
paying customers to relieve the grid, explicitly to defer infrastructure
investment, and Danish DSOs are developing conditional connections in congested
areas.

One number worth carrying into the Icelandic argument: in the Cerius-Radius
network, constrained substations require demand reduction during only
**approximately 3–4% of annual hours at most**. That is the quantified form of
the report's §5.1 argument — capacity scarcity is a small number of hours, not
a permanent condition, and pricing it as permanent is the error.

### Finland

**Helen Sähköverkko with Fingrid** launched the first live local flexibility
marketplace pilot in **winter 2025**.

### Iceland — the gap

No Icelandic flexibility market, conditional connection framework, or DSO
flexibility procurement was found in this research.

Landsnet presented on **Demand Side Response in Iceland** to the EU
Clean Energy for EU Islands secretariat (2023). The document is a
scanned/graphical PDF and could not be read by the tools available — **it should
be retrieved and read manually; it is the single most relevant Icelandic
source found.**

What is documented: Iceland's transmission system has extensive WAMPAC coverage
with roughly **110 PMUs** and multiple wide-area control schemes. So the TSO has
sophisticated monitoring and control at transmission level — the gap is at
distribution, and specifically in the *market and contractual* layer, not the
measurement layer.

**Inference:** Iceland is the only Nordic country without a conditional
connection framework or flexibility market activity. That absence is itself the
finding, and it is a stronger framing than "Iceland should try this" — it is
"Iceland is the Nordic outlier, and the EU instrument that obliges the others is
EEA-relevant."

## The Netherlands — the operational benchmark

Not Scandinavian, but the most advanced regime in Europe and the closest match
to the Vík expansion case.

Since **1 April 2025**, Dutch network operators offer a choice of connection and
transport agreement types:

| Instrument | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Firm CTA (FA)** | Full contracted capacity guaranteed at all times |
| **Non-firm CTA-85 (NFA85)** | Operator may reduce capacity within an **annual time budget** |
| **Capaciteitsbeperkingscontract (CBC)** | Capacity-limiting contract, separate from the 24/7 firm contract, typically running until local grid expansion completes |
| **Time-based transmission right** | Access by time of day |
| **Time-block-based transmission right** | Access within agreed time blocks — available on the **regional medium-voltage grid** |

ACM approved the two new alternative transmission rights following a joint
proposal from all Dutch grid operators via Netbeheer Nederland.

**Why this matters for Vík:** the NFA85 *annual time budget* is the mechanism
that answers RARIK's likely objection — that non-firm access is unbounded risk
for the customer. A bounded, contractual, annual curtailment budget makes the
product financeable. That is the design detail the Icelandic report currently
lacks, and it is directly liftable.

Also relevant: **trials of time-dependent transport rights (TDTR)** offered by
the Dutch TSO, and the observation that grid operators in some European
countries already offer **CPOs specifically** flexible connection contracts
allocating less capacity at morning/evening peak and more midday.

## Protocols — what is actually deployed

This is where the research is least conclusive, and it matters for the north-side
choice.

**OpenADR in Europe.** The Alliance's own material describes European activity
as growing — UK, Germany, Netherlands, Croatia — and claims OpenADR is "fast
becoming the most common standard for EV charging demand response
applications." **Evidence class: vendor/consortium claim, not independently
verified.** No evidence was found in this research of a *Nordic DSO* operating a
production OpenADR VTN.

**OSCP** remains the protocol actually designed for the DSO→CPO case: a
24-hour forecast of available grid capacity, on 15-minute intervals, with the
CPO adjusting charging profiles within it. The functional distinction found in
the sources: **OSCP forecasts available capacity; OpenADR gives the DSO more
direct control of equipment, including the ability to curtail directly.**

For a DSO issuing a binding envelope, that distinction favours OpenADR. For a
DSO publishing a day-ahead capacity forecast, it favours OSCP. Worth stating
explicitly in the report rather than treating OSCP's dormancy at OCA as
disqualifying.

**IEC 60870-5-104 vs IEC 61850.** 104 remains in widespread use for
substation→control-centre telemetry and "perfectly meets operators' current
communication needs"; 61850 is the richer substation-automation standard and
utilities expect RTU vendors to begin discontinuing 104 support. Neither is a
DSO→CPO commercial interface. This supports the report's §8.4 boundary table:
these live below the connection point, not across it.

**OCPP 2.1 was released January 2025** with DER control and V2X support. That is
directly relevant — it means the southbound leg of the architecture gains
standardised DER control without waiting for anything, and it is worth checking
whether Driivz or AMPECO have 2.1 on a roadmap.

**Dynamic operating envelopes** are described in the current literature as
day-ahead signals from DSO to flexible customers such as EV aggregators, who
adjust next-day scheduling. Note the cadence: **day-ahead, not real-time.** The
Vík test is designed around near-real-time envelope changes, which is *more*
demanding than mainstream European practice — worth knowing so the proof isn't
undersold.

## What this means for the Icelandic argument

Five changes I would make to the technical report on the strength of this:

1. **Lead with Article 6a, not California.** The report's §14.3 questions to
   Landsnet and §15 counterargument table both get materially stronger if the
   opening premise is "the EU has legislated this and the instrument is
   EEA-relevant" rather than "California does it."

2. **Replace the CSIP-AUS/Flex Connect precedents with Norway and the
   Netherlands as primary.** Norway for the legal instrument, the Netherlands
   for the product design. Keep California and Australia as secondary.

3. **Adopt the annual curtailment budget.** The Dutch NFA85 mechanism is the
   missing piece in §10's four-product split — it bounds the customer's
   downside and makes non-firm access financeable rather than merely cheaper.

4. **Use the Danish 3–4% figure.** "Constrained substations in Denmark's
   largest DSO network need demand reduction in 3–4% of annual hours" is the
   most compact statement of the report's central thesis found in this research.

5. **Reframe Iceland's position.** Not "Iceland should pilot this" but "Iceland
   is the Nordic outlier — conditional connections are used or piloted in every
   other Nordic country, Sweden has permanent solutions, Norway has a named
   regulation and a standard contract, and the EU instrument obliging the rest
   is EEA-relevant."

## What I could not establish

Stated plainly so nothing here is over-claimed:

- **EEA incorporation status of Directive 2024/1711.** EEA-relevant ≠ in force
  in Iceland. Must be checked directly. **This is the single most important
  open question in this note.**
- **Landsnet's actual DSR position.** The 2023 presentation is a graphical PDF
  the tooling could not read. Retrieve and read manually.
- **Whether any Nordic DSO runs OpenADR in production.** Nothing found either
  way. The absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, but it does mean the
  "everyone uses OpenADR" claim cannot be made.
- **RARIK's or Orkustofnun's published position on flexibility**, if any.
- **Norwegian NEM §3-2 exact wording** — the provision was identified but the
  Lovdata text was not read in full.
- Three sources returned HTTP 403 or were unreadable scanned PDFs (Nordic
  Energy Research chapter pages, the Swedish Ei literature review, the Landsnet
  presentation). Their content here comes from search summaries rather than
  primary reading, and is marked accordingly.
